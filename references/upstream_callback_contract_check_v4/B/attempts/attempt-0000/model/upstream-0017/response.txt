We study polynomial degree and coefficient regularization within the host's fixed regression implementation. Let \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{80}\) denote the supplied training observations and let \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}=\{(x_j^{\mathrm{val}},y_j^{\mathrm{val}})\}_{j=1}^{64}\) denote the supplied validation observations. A candidate is specified by \(c=(d,\alpha)\), where \(d\in\{1,\ldots,8\}\) and \(\alpha\in[0,100]\). Its fitted predictor is defined operationally as
\[
\widehat f_{d,\alpha}
=
\operatorname{Fit}_{\mathrm{host}}
\bigl(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}};d,\alpha\bigr).
\]
This notation preserves the host's feature expansion, intercept treatment, feature scaling, loss normalization, and numerical solver without assigning undocumented conventions to the execution. In the conventional polynomial representation introduced in Background, the predictor has the form \(b+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\beta_k x^k\), and ridge fitting combines squared residuals with a quadratic coefficient penalty. At \(\alpha=0.0\), the penalty vanishes, yielding an unpenalized fit in the specified feature space. The executed starting candidate is \(c_0=(2,0.0)\). Quadratic degree is motivated by apparent curvature in the supplied training observations; this motivation does not identify the population regression function or establish that a quadratic model is preferable to other degrees. All coefficient fitting uses the training split exclusively. Validation responses enter the comparison criterion, rather than the coefficient-fitting operation. No additional observations, alternative estimators, or modifications to the host implementation are introduced. Joint consideration of degree and regularization follows the covariance-dependent interpretation of ridge estimation described in the supplied synopsis of \textit{Ridge Regression: Structure, Cross-Validation, and Sketching} (arXiv 1910.02373v2).

The proposed comparison first evaluates the finite candidate set
\[
\mathcal{C}_0
=
\bigl(\{1,2,3,4\}\times\{0.0,0.1,1.0\}\bigr)
\;\cup\;
\bigl(\{5,6,7,8\}\times\{0.1,1.0\}\bigr).

This set contains 20 distinct configurations, including the already measured starting candidate. Degrees 1 through 4 provide a direct comparison between linear, quadratic, and moderately more flexible predictors, while positive regularization at degrees 5 through 8 examines whether additional polynomial terms are useful when their coefficients are penalized. The omission of unpenalized degrees 5 through 8 limits the comparison; it is a stated design choice rather than evidence that those configurations perform poorly. After host measurements are available for \(\mathcal{C}_0\), the protocol identifies the degree \(d_0\) of the best first-stage candidate and proposes a second-stage regularization set
\[
\mathcal{A}_1=\{0.01,0.03,0.3,3.0,10.0,100.0\},

\mathcal{C}_1=\{d_0\}\times\mathcal{A}_1.

Previously evaluated configurations are not repeated. The full planned comparison therefore contains 26 distinct candidates, of which only one currently has supplied execution evidence. The additional regularization values examine weaker shrinkage, intermediate shrinkage, and stronger penalties up to the permitted upper bound. Their numerical values are meaningful under the retained host conventions. This staged design concentrates subsequent evaluations on the degree preferred by the initial comparison, but it does not explore every degree at every second-stage regularization value. Consequently, a degree rejected in the first stage could still perform better at an unexamined strength. Neither the finite grid nor its adaptive extension establishes a global optimum over the permitted continuous regularization interval.

For every successfully evaluated candidate, the primary comparison statistic is the host-measured validation mean squared error,
\[
V(d,\alpha)
=
\frac{1}{64}
\sum_{j=1}^{64}
\left[
y_j^{\mathrm{val}}
-
\widehat f_{d,\alpha}(x_j^{\mathrm{val}})
\right]^2.
\]
The corresponding training statistic,
\[
T(d,\alpha)
=
\frac{1}{80}
\sum_{i=1}^{80}
\left[y_i-\widehat f_{d,\alpha}(x_i)\right]^2,
\]
is reported descriptively and does not determine hyperparameter selection. Let \(\mathcal{E}\) contain only candidates with supplied valid host measurements. Selection orders candidates by the tuple \((V(d,\alpha),d,\alpha)\): lower validation MSE takes precedence, followed by lower degree and then lower regularization strength in exact ties. Formally,
\[
(d^\star,\alpha^\star)
=
\mathop{\operatorname{arg\,min}}_{(d,\alpha)\in\mathcal{E}}^{\mathrm{lex}}
\bigl(V(d,\alpha),d,\alpha\bigr),
\]
where \(\mathrm{lex}\) denotes lexicographic ordering. The same rule determines \(d_0\) within the first-stage set once its evaluations are complete. Comparisons use the supplied metric precision before presentation rounding; values that merely round to the same displayed number are not treated as exact ties. Each planned candidate is submitted separately through a literal configuration containing only degree and regularization strength, and fitting and metric computation remain the trusted host's responsibility. Proposed candidates, failed evaluations, and configurations lacking execution records cannot enter \(\mathcal{E}\). Maintaining this distinction prevents a written experimental plan from being interpreted as a completed search.

The present evidence supports \(\mathcal{E}=\{(2,0.0)\}\). The singleton selection retains the measured quadratic candidate but provides no empirical comparison between degrees or regularization strengths. Future results should associate each configuration with its own host record and distinguish completed evaluations from pending candidates. Because the second-stage degree and final configuration depend on the same validation responses, their selected validation error is also affected by the search procedure. Repeated comparison can favor sample-specific fluctuations, and the supplied literature discussion of validation bias does not quantify that effect for this dataset. No independent test assessment, repeated-split analysis, confidence interval, or significance test is available. The method therefore supports a reproducible candidate comparison under a common host implementation, while reserving comparative and generalization conclusions for the measurements required to establish them.
